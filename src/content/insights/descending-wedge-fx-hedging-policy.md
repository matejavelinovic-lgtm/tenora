Most CFOs understand what currency volatility can do to a business’s margins, cash flow and budget. Most also know they should hedge to protect against it. The harder questions are how much to hedge, and how far ahead. A descending wedge answers both, with a policy simple enough to explain to a board in a few minutes.

### What it is

A descending wedge sets a minimum and maximum hedge level for each month ahead, typically over 12 months, though the horizon depends on the business. A policy could also use quarters rather than months.

Hedge levels are highest in the near term, where forecasts are most reliable, and taper off further out, where they are less certain. Plotted, the levels form a wedge that descends over time.

It is a rolling policy. Month 1 is always the current month. As time moves on, each exposure moves into a nearer month and its required hedging rises.

The minimum is the critical cover the business always holds. The maximum is the most it can hold. The gap between them is where the risk manager can act on market conditions.

### Who it suits

A descending wedge works best where foreign currency flows are regular and can be forecast with reasonable confidence. Typical examples:

- importers paying overseas suppliers
- exporters with recurring foreign currency sales
- businesses with overseas payroll or operating costs
- businesses with foreign currency subscription or contract revenue

It also suits finance teams that need to show clearly why hedging decisions were made, in a way that is straightforward to manage and to explain to a board, auditors or investors.

It is less suited to one-off or irregular exposures, such as an acquisition, a large capital purchase or a single contract. Those are usually hedged individually against the specific deal.

### Keeping volatility within risk appetite

Every FX policy starts with risk appetite: how far below budget the business is willing to let its foreign currency cash flows fall. In a descending wedge policy, the proposed hedge levels are stress tested against this limit using simulated FX volatility and adjusted until the modelled shortfalls stay within it.

For example, an Australian company expecting AUD 15m from its US dollar revenue might set a risk appetite of 5%, or AUD 750k. Unhedged, in 95% of modelled scenarios the shortfall stays under AUD 2m. With the right policy in place, that worst case loss falls to around AUD 730k, inside appetite.

That testing is done through scenario modelling, which runs the policy through a range of possible currency movements to show how it holds up. The results are a guide rather than a prediction. The aim is hedging levels that are approximately right rather than precisely wrong.

### The risks of having no policy

Without a policy, hedging decisions tend to follow the market rather than the business. Cover gets added when rates look good and held back when they don’t, in the hope of a better rate. That turns hedging into a bet on currency direction, which is the very risk hedging is meant to remove.

That approach exposes the business in three ways:

1. **Under-hedging.** Waiting for a better rate can leave flows uncovered when the market moves the other way. As the example shows, that can push the shortfall well outside appetite.
2. **Over-hedging.** Locking in large volumes when a rate looks attractive can mean hedging flows that never materialise, which can create outright losses. Longer-dated hedges also have more time to move out of the money, which can trigger margin calls depending on the instrument used.
3. **Inconsistency.** Decisions vary with whoever makes them and how they read the market at the time. That makes them hard to explain to a board or auditor when the time comes.

### Clear strategy, human oversight and real-time data working together

In practice, running the policy needs three things.

- The first is an up-to-date view of cover against each level, by currency and by month.
- The second is a clear owner with the authority to top up cover when it falls below the minimum, or hold back as it nears the maximum.
- The third is a routine for updating that view when forecasts change, because a revised forecast changes how much cover each month needs.

With those in place, the policy does what it was set up to do: keep currency risk within appetite, with every decision led by a clear rule.
